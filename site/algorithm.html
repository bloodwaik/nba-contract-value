How it works

From box score to surplus value

The model has one job: predict what fraction of the salary cap each player would command on the open market. The gap between that and what they actually make is the contract's surplus value.

The five-step recipe

No black box
1

Normalize salaries

Raw dollars rot — a $30M deal in 2019 is not a $30M deal in 2026. Every contract is converted to cap_pct = salary / season_cap, putting every era on the same scale.

cap_pct = salary ÷ season_cap
2

Build the feature stack

Curated stats only — no PPG/RPG noise. Impact (BPM, DWS), efficiency × volume (TS%, USG%, AST%), hidden value (STL%, ORB%), availability (total minutes), demographics (age, age², position bucket). Box scores feed in, latent player value comes out.

3

Fit market rate

A Ridge regression learns the open-market exchange rate between each production unit and cap space. Ridge over OLS because BPM, DWS, VORP, TS%, USG% are correlated and we want stable coefficients. All features are standardized so the coefficients are directly comparable.

Expected_cap_pct = β₀ + β·features + ε
4

Score every player

Run every active player through the trained model to get their expected cap percentage. This is the model's read on what they would command on the open market today given their production.

5

Compute surplus & rank

Subtract actual cap hit from expected and multiply by the current cap to convert to dollars. Positive = team profit; negative = the contract is upside-down on the market.

surplus_$ = (expected_cap_pct − actual_cap_pct) × season_cap

What the market pays

Learned coefficients · standardized features

Each bar is the marginal cap-percentage the model assigns per one-standard-deviation increase in that feature, holding the others fixed. Positive bars mean the market pays more for that input; negative bars mean it discounts it.

Note: age and age² are highly correlated by construction — Ridge spreads the curve across both. The net age effect is a quadratic peak in the late 20s, not what either bar shows alone.

Inputs used

Why each one earns its place

Impact metrics

BPM (Box Plus/Minus) and DWS (Defensive Win Shares). BPM is the gold-standard free EPM substitute. DWS prevents the model from systematically underpaying elite defenders who don't shoot a lot.

Efficiency × volume

TS% combines 2s, 3s, and FTs into one efficiency number. USG% says how many possessions a player consumes. AST% captures shot creation for others. The intersection of high TS% × high USG% is the most expensive thing in basketball.

Hidden value

STL% and ORB%. Steals are weighted ~9× points in some impact frameworks. Offensive rebounds from non-centers extend possessions and quietly drive surplus.

Availability & age

Total minutes penalize load-managed stars. Games played sanity-checks distribution. Age (and age²) lets the model trace an aging curve — peak around 27, decline after 30.

Known limits

  • Supermax ceiling. Predictions are clipped at 35% of the cap (the league's supermax). Truly elite players (Jokić, SGA, Wemby) get floored at the ceiling, which suppresses their measured surplus.
  • Veteran-minimum floor. Predictions are clipped at 1% of the cap. Bench-end production can't "earn" less than the league min by construction.
  • EPM substitute. EPM is paywalled at Dunks & Threes. BPM is the free stand-in. Swap any impact metric into features.py and the rest of the pipeline runs unchanged.
  • Training set. This demo trains in-sample. The cleanest version trains on prior-season → cap_pct at the moment of signing, across many seasons. The pipeline supports it; you just feed a different DataFrame.
  • Injury history. The model rewards minutes played but doesn't penalize multi-year injury history. Easy one-line extension.
